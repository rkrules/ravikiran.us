# The Long Way Up: Ten Days Chasing Altitude Through Colorado

We landed in Denver a little before midnight, into the kind of turbulence that keeps a
flight crew belted in for the whole descent, and spent our first ninety minutes on the
ground not in the mountains but in a rental lot — sent first to one open-bed pickup,
then another, before someone finally produced the modest SUV we'd booked months
earlier. It was not an auspicious start. It was, in hindsight, exactly right: this was a
trip that never quite went the way it was planned, and was better for it.

Ravi, our daughter Arya, and I had come to loop counterclockwise out of Denver — up into
Rocky Mountain National Park, down through Glenwood Springs to the Maroon Bells, across
to the Black Canyon of the Gunnison, and back around by way of Pikes Peak and the Garden
of the Gods. For much of the route we traveled alongside Ravi's cousin Roshan, his wife
Priya, and their fifteen-month-old daughter Mudita — two families, one grade-schooler,
one toddler, two grandparents, and a rhythm that set in almost immediately: drive
somewhere high, hike until the legs gave out, find something hot to eat, sleep hard,
repeat.

## What altitude actually does to a body

Nobody had told us, in any way that landed, what nine or ten thousand feet would feel
like. We found out on our second day, when a "short" hike into Rocky Mountain National
Park — out to Ouzel Falls and back, past Calypso Cascades and its bridge over fallen
logs — turned into six miles and four hours, in city clothes we'd packed for a gentle
afternoon in Boulder. Arya's stomach turned queasy on every uphill push; my head swam
whenever I moved too fast. We kept going anyway, because every hiker coming the other
way swore we were "almost there," and because Ouzel Falls, when we reached it in a fine
mist, was worth the argument with our own bodies to get there.

By dinner Arya was too sick to manage a single chip, until a waitress in Estes Park
handed her a soda with fresh lime and a pinch of salt — a local trick that worked, every
single time, for the rest of the trip. We never scheduled a hike that ambitious on an
arrival day again. The mountains reward patience and punish confidence, and the first
lesson of Colorado is to let your lungs and your itinerary catch up with each other.

## A ranger badge, a shed antler, and a marmot with no fear of people

What redeemed the hard mornings were the small, specific wonders that a national park
hands you without warning: a volunteer at Sheep Lakes letting Arya hold a thirty-pound
shed elk antler and explaining, patiently, the difference between antlers that grow back
every year carrying blood and the horns of bighorn sheep, which are keratin and stay for
life. Arya recited the whole lesson back that evening, word for word, and finished her
Junior Ranger booklet the same day — sworn in on the spot at the Alpine Visitor Center,
the highest in the country, after a ranger talk on how far to stand from a moose.

Two days later, at Emerald Lake at the top of the Bear Lake Corridor, a marmot walked
right up to where we sat and a pair of Clark's nutcrackers worked the rocks close enough
to touch. We had earned the view — Nymph Lake's lily pads, then Dream Lake under the
peaks, then the last rocky push to Emerald — and we sat there for the better part of an
hour, not talking about much, which is its own kind of achievement with a
seven-year-old.

## The bells, the burn, and the best evening of the trip

Aspen's Maroon Bells are reached by shuttle, and the day we went it was ninety-five
degrees at the trailhead. Three fourteen-thousand-foot peaks — Maroon Peak, North Maroon
Peak, and Pyramid Peak — stood over Crater Lake, which in that dry summer had pulled
back to a third of its usual size; it hardly mattered, with chipmunks running raids on
our packed lunch and the peaks doing what they were there to do. We came down sunburnt
and moved fast, an hour and twenty minutes on legs that had stopped complaining out of
sheer resignation.

That evening undid all of it: Iron Mountain Hot Springs, a hillside of small pools each
held at its own temperature, named for gemstones, the hottest near a hundred and eight
degrees. We soaked for two hours and watched the sun go down over the Colorado River
from the water. If the trip had a single hour worth repeating on its own, this was it.

## Two rims, a wildfire, and a bakery that felt like home

The Black Canyon of the Gunnison split neatly into two moods. The North Rim, reached
through the town of Paonia — where a closed Thai food truck sent us instead to a bakery
called Clean Kitchen, run by a cook who had once lived a few miles from where I grew up
in Hartford — was quiet almost to the point of solitude: a small ranger station staffed
by a retired couple volunteering for the summer, a walk to Exclamation Point through
sagebrush with Canada jays trailing us the whole way, and the smell of a wildfire
burning near Lake City drifting faint through the canyon air. The South Rim, two and a
half hours around by road though only a few miles across the gorge, was the famous side
— Painted Wall, Rock Point, overlook after overlook onto the same impossible drop, each
one demanding its own photograph.

## The summit, the donuts, and going home

We closed the loop at Pikes Peak, driven rather than climbed, on a timed-entry ticket
with a mandatory brake check on the way down — the descent runs hot enough to need it.
Arya's ears popped and her stomach turned somewhere past twelve thousand feet, and we
nearly gave up a few switchbacks from the top, until the summit produced a slice of
cheese pizza and one of the mountain's famous high-altitude donuts and fixed everything
at once. Inside the visitor center we learned that Zebulon Pike himself never reached
this summit, that the people who lived here first called it the mountain of the sun, and
that "America the Beautiful" was written from this exact view — a nice thing to learn
standing in the wind, looking down at the Garden of the Gods far below.

Ten days, four mountain towns, roughly nine hundred miles on the odometer, six national
park hikes, one hot spring, one summit, and a child who now insists, with the authority
of a sworn Junior Ranger, that the correct word for animal droppings is "scat." We flew
home still tasting the altitude — and already talking about where the next loop should
go.
